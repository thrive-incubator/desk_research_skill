## Current snapshot

Early childhood compensation is the sector's defining operational constraint, and it is not moving:
Brookings found average hourly wages for child care workers reached **$22.15** in 2025 dollars, up
from $14.25 in the early 1990s, while the gap against all women widened from about **$20,000
annually in 1990-1995 to $28,000 by 2020-2025** — child care workers still earn roughly **$8,000
less annually** than other low-wage workers despite being better educated (66% with some college vs.
52%). A Harvard analysis reported in August 2026 argues the answer is simply to pay workers more.
The federal lever that would have forced the issue is being withdrawn: the pending workforce final
rule would remove the 2024 wage-comparability and benefits requirements
(see [[hspps-workforce-rule]]).

**So programs are routing around wages.** The clearest instance is Friends Center for Children in
New Haven, which houses **10 of its 47 teachers for free** and is targeting **30% of its teaching
staff by 2028**. Its CEO concluded in 2019 that the organization could not budget salary increases
without more public funding or higher tuition, and went after teachers' largest expense instead.
The economics behind that choice: Connecticut early childhood educators earn about **$31,000 a
year** against a state median rent of about **$24,000 a year**. Housing comes from donated
properties plus a Yale School of Architecture partnership building roughly one home a year — homes
worth about **$750,000** acquired for about **$300,000**. Every housed teacher meets **monthly with
a financial coach**. More than **1,000 visitors** have come to see it since 2020, and the model is
replicating in **Missouri, Michigan and Georgia**, including a two-teacher pilot at Urban Sprouts in
St. Louis. Federal tax policy is nibbling at the same problem from the other end via the **SEED
Act**'s $350 above-the-line deduction (see [[seed-act]]).

**The 2026-W35 window added a second non-wage lever and a leadership-pipeline problem.** The Urban
Institute argued that guaranteeing child care assistance to **early educators who are themselves
parents of young children** would help address the US child care shortage — a retention mechanism
that costs a program less than a wage increase and can be administered with a tool rather than a
budget amendment, which makes it the most directly product-shaped compensation lever on this page.
Separately, the field's **policy leadership pipeline** surfaced as its own constraint: Michael H.
Levine of ECPIHE (Early Childhood Policy in Institutions of Higher Education, housed at Teachers
College, Columbia University) argued the field lacks trained leaders able to translate policy into
working systems — "early childhood policy decisions are made every day by people who care deeply
about children but were prepared to do something else entirely." ECPIHE spans **17 institutions of
higher education**, and a recent Washington, D.C. gathering drew **56 graduate students and faculty
from 12 universities**, with participants drawn from Head Start and child care centers, social work,
health care, and home visiting.

**Entry-strategy read.** Compensation is capped by the funding formula; **benefits, logistics and
financial-wellbeing supports are not** — and under the HSPPS rescission's proposed 5% administrative
cost cap, a benefit that reads as a program cost is structurally advantaged over one that reads as
overhead (see [[hspps-rescission-nprm]]). Products that reduce a teacher's cost of living or cost of
working — financial coaching, benefits navigation, child care assistance for educator-parents,
transportation, credentialing pathways — sell into the one lever a program director still controls.
Note the diffusion pattern, though: 1,000 site visits and a peer-to-peer replication chain, with no
vendor anywhere in it. This field adopts from other practitioners, not from sales motions — and
ECPIHE's 17 institutions are the concrete surface for an incubator recruiting policy-fluent talent
rather than selling to it. *(as of 2026-W35)*
