## Current snapshot

A procurement model is spreading in which part of a vendor's payment depends on whether students
actually use the service and hit agreed goals. **Michigan now requires the approach to access $50
million for virtual tutoring**, and 20-plus states have adopted some version, with early adopters
including California, Florida, Mississippi and Texas. A WestEd evaluation covering eight districts
across those four states from August 2024 to March 2026 looked at **10 interventions but could
rigorously assess only 4**; **3 of those showed measurable academic gains**. AI-powered reading
software significantly improved second-grade proficiency but showed no benefit for older elementary
students. There was **no evidence of cost savings** — overall spending resembled traditional
contracts. The Center for Outcomes Based Contracting spent roughly **$80,000 per district** in
coaching and technical assistance and is now testing models under $10,000. In the same week, a major
virtual tutoring provider shut down with experts citing a lack of evidence.

The evidence is K-12 so far; the relevance is that Head Start is being pushed toward the same place
from the opposite direction. When the HSPPS stops naming the instrument and the mandate becomes a
local budget decision, the surviving basis for a sale is demonstrated effect rather than compliance
alignment (see [[hspps-rescission-nprm]]). Two findings travel: the **youngest-grade result was the
strongest one**, and **only 4 of 10 interventions could be evaluated rigorously at all** — which
makes measurability a product requirement rather than an evaluation afterthought.

**2026-W35 added the supply side of that evidence bar.** Two peer-reviewed papers registered in the
August 20-27 window bear directly on what a vendor will be asked to produce. A **synthesis of
experimental research** on design principles and pedagogical practices in literacy apps for young
children is now the evidence tier an education manager can cite against an early-literacy product —
a synthesis of experimental studies outranks any vendor-run efficacy study in a procurement
challenge. And a **systematic review of early childhood educators' data practices**, "From Data
Collection to Data Use," goes at the claim every early-childhood data platform makes and rarely
evidences: that it closes the gap between collecting data and using it. Both were registered without
a deposited abstract, so they are recorded at title level. Either can function as the best available
citation for a pitch or the most credible objection to it — which is the point: under outcomes-based
purchasing, the literature is the counterparty's ammunition as much as the vendor's.
*(as of 2026-W35)*
