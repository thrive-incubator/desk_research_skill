## Current snapshot

The K-12 revenue base is shrinking structurally, not cyclically: district leaders rank budget
shortfalls and declining enrollment as their top concerns, against an analysis projecting **$11.5
billion annually in lost per-pupil funding by 2030-31**. W31 showed what that does to wellbeing
staffing — Chicago closing a **$732.5M deficit** inside a nearly $10B budget with a sub-1% net staffing
change that concealed 200+ schools gaining positions and ~270 losing them, mandated special-education
roles growing while discretionary counseling absorbed the cuts.

**W34 shows the next move: districts stop absorbing and start asking voters.** Denver states the
mechanism explicitly — about **89,000 students on a $1.5 billion budget**, with declining enrollment
cutting revenue because "Colorado schools are funded per student, fewer kids means fewer dollars,"
compounded by rising employee health-insurance and utility costs and more students needing special
education. Its answer is a **$44 million mill levy override** on the November 3 ballot. Chicago's
answer is more precarious: after years of shrinking enrollments and a growing workforce, a board
majority **rejected the layoffs** interim CEO Macquline King proposed as "a responsible way to address
the district's deficits" and balanced instead on an assumed **$150 million in additional state
revenue** — with "disruptive midyear cuts" the stated consequence if the November veto session does not
deliver. It also put two non-binding revenue questions to voters and appointed a new chief lobbyist to
a post vacant more than a year. Board vice president Angel Velez framed the gap as the distance between
what Illinois' formula calls "adequate" and what CPS receives.

The pattern for the vault: **per-pupil funding under enrollment decline is a structural squeeze that
districts now answer with local tax measures or with a bet on the state** — see
[[local-tax-referendums]]. The first is recurring revenue restricted to educational programming; the
second is a single-date gamble. Vendors should read a district's choice between them as a direct
signal of contract risk.
*(as of 2026-W34)*

## Timeline

### 2026-W34 — Denver names the mechanism: per-student funding plus enrollment decline, answered with a $44M override
*Chalkbeat · August 14, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/colorado/2026/08/14/denver-voters-to-decide-on-44-million-mill-levy-override-for-public-schools/)
Denver Public Schools, the state's largest district with about 89,000 students and a $1.5 billion budget, placed a $44 million mill levy override on the November 3 ballot. Officials cited declining enrollment against Colorado's per-student funding formula, rising employee health insurance and utility costs, and an increase in students needing special education services. The measure includes $25.8 million in flat $1,750 raises, $4 million for CTE, $2.9 million for student mental health, $2 million for special education, and $9.3 million shared with district charter schools.

### 2026-W34 — Chicago rejects layoffs and balances on an unsecured $150M in state revenue
*Chalkbeat · August 13, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/chicago/2026/08/13/chicago-public-schools-board-backs-revenue-referendums/)
Weeks before placing two advisory revenue referendums on the November ballot, a Chicago Board of Education majority rejected the educator and staff layoffs in interim CEO Macquline King's budget proposal — which King had argued was a responsible response to deficits following years of shrinking enrollments and a growing workforce — and balanced the budget by assuming an influx of additional state revenue. The district must secure $150 million during the November veto session or likely face disruptive midyear cuts; it appointed Iris Millán as chief of intergovernmental affairs, a post vacant more than a year, to lead that effort.
