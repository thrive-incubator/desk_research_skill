## Current snapshot

**Post-ESSER, districts that can no longer fund student supports from formula dollars are asking
voters directly — which turns an election date into a procurement date.** W34 opens this arc with
three simultaneous cases, all pointing at **November 3, 2026**.

**Denver** is the cleanest instance of wellbeing money on a ballot. The school board voted unanimously
to place a **$44 million mill levy override** before voters, raising property taxes about $72 a year
on a median $624,800 home, with an itemized split: $25.8M for a flat $1,750 raise for every full-time
employee, $4M for career and technical education programming, **$2.9M to expand student mental health
support**, **$2M for special education services**, and $9.3M shared to district charter schools as
state law requires. DPS serves about 89,000 students on a $1.5 billion budget and cites declining
enrollment under per-student funding, rising health-insurance and utility costs, and more students
needing special education. The precedent is encouraging: Denver's 2020 override, $32 million, was
earmarked for the same categories — salaries, special education, mental health, school nurses — and
passed.

**Chicago** shows the weaker form. The board placed **two non-binding advisory questions** on the
ballot — a statewide millionaires tax for education, and a penalty on vacant-land owners with revenue
steered to CPS — neither of which compels anyone to act. It did so weeks after rejecting the layoffs
in interim CEO Macquline King's budget and instead balancing on an assumed **$150 million in
additional state revenue** that must be secured in the November veto session or the district faces
"disruptive midyear cuts." The Civic Federation and dissenting board member Jennifer Custer called
the referendums "window dressing."

**Indiana** shows the charter-sector consequence. Eight districts in Marion and Lake counties are
running referendums totalling nearly **$200 million maximum annually**, of which an estimated **$49.5
million** must be shared with about **60 charter schools** under state law — the largest amount
charters could receive from ballot questions in state history.

The pattern worth tracking: mill levy overrides are recurring, locally controlled, and legally
constrained to educational programming — a materially better durability profile than ESSER for
funding mental health and special education capacity. Advisory referendums are not funding at all.
Both are being used in the same season, and the difference between them is the difference between a
buyer and a press release.
*(as of 2026-W34)*

## Timeline

### 2026-W34 — Denver puts a $44M override on the ballot with $2.9M for mental health and $2M for special education
*Chalkbeat · August 14, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/colorado/2026/08/14/denver-voters-to-decide-on-44-million-mill-levy-override-for-public-schools/)
The Denver school board voted unanimously to place a $44 million mill levy override on the November 3 ballot, raising taxes about $72/year on a median $624,800 home: $25.8M for a flat $1,750 raise per full-time employee, $4M for CTE programming, $2.9M to expand student mental health support, $2M for special education, and $9.3M shared with district charter schools. DPS cited declining enrollment under per-student funding, rising insurance and utility costs, and growth in students needing special education. Denver's last override, $32M in 2020, was earmarked for the same categories.

### 2026-W34 — Chicago's board sends two advisory revenue questions to voters after betting the budget on $150M in state money
*Chalkbeat · August 13, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/chicago/2026/08/13/chicago-public-schools-board-backs-revenue-referendums/)
For the first time in its history the Chicago Board of Education voted to place two non-binding questions on the November ballot — a statewide millionaires tax for public education and a penalty on vacant-land owners with revenue directed to CPS. Neither compels action. The vote followed a board majority rejecting the layoffs in interim CEO Macquline King's budget and balancing instead on an assumed $150 million in additional state revenue that must be secured in the November veto session or the district faces disruptive midyear cuts.

### 2026-W34 — Indiana referendums would route an estimated $49.5M to about 60 charter schools
*Chalkbeat · August 18, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/indiana/2026/08/18/how-much-money-charter-schools-could-receive-from-2026-referendums/)
Eight Indiana districts in Marion and Lake counties are running November referendums totalling nearly $200 million maximum annually, of which an estimated $49.5 million must be shared with about 60 charter schools under state law — the largest sum charters could receive from ballot questions in state history. Only four of the eight districts filed individual charter spending plans with the state, and $48.7 million of the $49.5 million is unaccounted for in official plans (also: [Chalkbeat, August 17, 2026](https://www.chalkbeat.org/indiana/2026/08/17/indiana-school-district-referendum-voter-guide-2026-ballot-question/)).
