# Thrive K12 Funding & Wellbeing Synthesis
### Week of August 13 – August 20, 2026

## This Week's Throughline

- **The federal government spent the week writing the rules that decide which wellbeing products get bought — without appropriating a dollar.** ED's new edtech Dear Colleague Letter tells states and districts to put evidence of effectiveness *into procurement* and to stop measuring adoption by logins and screen time; OCR's discipline letter declares it a Title VI violation for a school to look at the racial outcomes of its discipline policy; and the FCC opened comment on whether to sunset E-rate, the fifth-largest federal funding stream schools receive. None of these is a grant. All three change what a district can buy and what it must be able to prove.
- **The most consequential item for anyone selling into early childhood is the Head Start proposed rule, and the detail that matters is buried.** More than 1,400 regulatory provisions are on the table, and the specific casualties include the monthly mental health consultation requirement and the fixed timelines for vision, hearing, immunization and well-child checks. Those requirements are the *compliance driver* that makes early-childhood screening and consultation procurable at all. Comment closes October 6.
- **Pennsylvania is the week's one clean instance of money flowing toward wellbeing:** $140 million made available for school safety and student mental health grants. Set that against Head Start deregulation, E-rate's possible sunset, and the Teen Pregnancy Prevention terminations now landing on named New York grantees ($4.3M/year, 19,000 young people) — the state channel keeps looking like the durable one, exactly as the Iowa and Chicago cases suggested a fortnight ago.
- **District demand for mental health capacity is now showing up on ballots rather than in budgets.** Denver's $44M mill levy override carves out $2.9M for mental health and $2M for special education; Chicago put two advisory revenue referendums up after refusing layoffs and betting on $150M in state money; Indiana's referendums would route an estimated $49.5M to about 60 charter schools. Districts that can't fund student supports from formula dollars are asking voters directly — which makes November 3 a real procurement date, not just an election.
- **A rare observed-procurement sighting:** an Oceanside, California elementary school named its actual SEL stack — Move This World for Tier 1, Riverside Insights' DESSA for screening, The Stepping Stones Group for clinical delivery. We almost never see the contract; this week we saw the vendor names.
- **Caveat, unchanged and worth repeating:** the district items below are budget and ballot *journalism*. We see referendum language and board votes; we do not see RFPs or awards. And the two Pennsylvania $140M reports reached us through Google News aggregation — the headline figure and program purpose are consistent across both, but we could not retrieve the underlying program detail.

## Federal Funding & Policy

### Education Department issues long-awaited edtech guidance, pushing evidence into procurement
*EdSurge · August 20, 2026* · [link](https://edsurge.com/news/department-of-education-issues-long-awaited-edtech-guidance-for-states-and-districts)

ED issued a Dear Colleague Letter on classroom educational technology but declined to write rules, expressly delegating implementation to states and districts. Assistant Secretary of Elementary and Secondary Education Kirsten Baesler wrote that technology use should be "purposeful, instructionally sound, and contributing to measurable student outcomes," and that "responsible design is the floor." The letter encourages states and districts to distinguish "recreational" from "instructional" technology, to evaluate tools on outcomes rather than screen time, and to **incorporate evidence of effectiveness into procurement, especially products that have conducted randomized controlled trials**. It asks vendors to publish independent evaluations "whenever feasible" and to minimize unnecessary screen exposure. The guidance praised Arkansas, Indiana, Louisiana, Michigan and Texas for "exploring contracting models" that rely on performance measures rather than adoption or usage metrics.

**So what:** This is the federal government endorsing outcomes-based contracting by name — the same purchasing model WestEd found effective and that has now spread to 87 school systems. For a wellbeing product with real efficacy evidence, this is the most favorable procurement signal of the year. For one without, the guidance is a slowly closing door.

### FCC opens comment on whether to limit or sunset E-rate, schools' fifth-largest federal funding stream
*K-12 Dive · August 18, 2026* · [link](https://www.k12dive.com/news/fcc-opens-comment-period-on-e-rates-future/828069/)

The FCC is accepting public comment on a proposed rule, published in the Federal Register on August 14, that weighs changes to or "discontinuation" of E-rate — asking whether the program "should be limited or sunset" given "today's extensive connectivity rates," and whether policy changes are needed in light of screen-time concerns in schools. E-rate accounted for $10.5 billion to school districts between 2021 and 2025 and is the fifth-largest pot of federal funds schools receive; nearly 25,000 districts, schools, consortiums and libraries received $13.2 billion in total over that period. The proposed rule also asks whether schools should receive E-rate on condition that they "provide parents with a meaningful opportunity to opt their children out of screen-based instruction or screen use during the school day." Comment closes October 13. The Education and Libraries Network Coalition, co-chaired by AASA, called on the FCC to focus on strengthening rather than terminating the program; AASA will host a virtual town hall on September 9 (also: [The 74, August 19, 2026](https://www.the74million.org/article/fcc-review-could-end-school-internet-program-advocates-plan-very-loud-protest/)).

**So what:** Any cloud-delivered wellbeing tool — telehealth, screening, SEL platform — runs on connectivity E-rate paid for. The parental screen-time opt-out condition is the more immediate threat: a district that must offer opt-outs from screen-based instruction becomes a much harder buyer for anything delivered on a device.

### Head Start proposed rule would strip the mental health consultation and screening timelines that make services procurable
*The 74 / The 19th · August 16, 2026* · [link](https://www.the74million.org/zero2eight/head-start-could-soon-be-overhauled-heres-exactly-what-would-change/)

The proposed rule released the prior week puts more than 1,400 regulatory provisions governing Head Start implementation on the chopping block, across 1,600 grant recipients. Specific removals: Head Start's teacher-to-student ratios (an estimated 80% of currently enrolled children would be subject to worse ratios, with maximum toddlers-per-teacher more than doubling in Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Nevada, North Carolina and Texas); the cap on group sizes; the prohibition on expelling children; detailed lead-exposure requirements; the bus monitor requirement; and the fixed timelines for health checks — insurance or medical care within 30 days of enrollment, vision and hearing screening within 45 days, immunization and well-child and dental checks within 90 days. **Many mental health supports, such as the monthly mental health consultation, would go away.** All instruction would have to be in English, affecting the more than a quarter of Head Start children from homes where another language is spoken; Early Head Start would lose the two-week newborn home visit deadline and the requirement to track and close racial and ethnic disparities in birth outcomes. Parent committees become optional. ACF Assistant Secretary Alex Adams framed it as reducing paperwork and giving flexibility on "personnel and workforce decisions"; HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said "it is important to protect" Head Start and wants more nutrition and physical activity requirements. Comment closes October 6. (also: [K-12 Dive, August 19, 2026](https://www.k12dive.com/news/how-would-head-start-programs-fare-if-federal-standards-are-pared-down/828173/); [The 74, August 19, 2026](https://www.the74million.org/zero2eight/head-start-providers-describe-a-system-in-turmoil-amid-federal-cutbacks/))

**So what:** This is the single most portfolio-relevant regulatory event of the month for early childhood. The performance standards are not red tape to a vendor — they are the *reason a program buys*. Remove the monthly mental health consultation requirement and the 45-day screening deadline, and consultation and screening become discretionary line items in 1,600 independently-managed budgets. Any early-childhood wellbeing thesis priced against the current HSPPS floor needs re-underwriting before the October 6 comment close.

### OCR tells schools to "stop racial balancing," calling outcome-based discipline review a Title VI violation
*K-12 Dive · August 18, 2026* · [link](https://www.k12dive.com/news/ocr-calls-on-schools-to-stop-racial-balancing-in-new-discipline-guidance/828211/)

ED's Office for Civil Rights issued a Dear Colleague Letter on school discipline stating that "it is race discrimination under Title VI for the school to evaluate the racial outcomes of their discipline policies and to make decisions based on or because of those racial outcomes." Assistant Secretary for Civil Rights Kimberly Richey wrote that neither Title VI nor the Constitution prohibits unintentional disparate impact or requires schools to consider race in discipline. The letter follows the administration's formal undoing of the disparate impact legal theory last month, and criticizes the Obama-era 2014 discipline guidance. Dear Colleague letters convey enforcement approach but do not carry the force of regulation.

**So what:** Disparate-impact review was the compliance hook that sold a great deal of behavioral and restorative-practice software — districts bought discipline analytics partly to defend against OCR investigations. That driver is now not merely gone but recast as itself a legal risk. Products whose pitch is "find your discipline disparities" need a new one; the underlying need (fewer exclusions, better behavioral support) hasn't changed, but the buying justification has.

### Teen Pregnancy Prevention terminations land on named New York grantees: $4.3M a year, 19,000 young people
*Chalkbeat / New York Focus · August 14, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/08/14/trump-administration-cuts-sex-education-funding/)

Of 67 federal Teen Pregnancy Prevention grants nationwide, all but 14 have been terminated as HHS shifts toward abstinence-based initiatives. Three New York grantees are losing over $4.3 million in annual federal funding that supported evidence-based sexual health education for over 19,000 young people a year: Children's Aid (a $936,700 annual grant, terminated effective immediately two years before expiry, with HHS citing lack of alignment with its priorities), NYC Teens Connection (losing $3.9 million over two years, 15 staff at risk; the program has worked with over 400 organizations and served nearly 130,000 teenagers since 2010), and HOPE Buffalo run by Cicatelli Associates ($1.4 million annually; credited with a 36% decline in teen pregnancy rates in high-poverty Buffalo neighborhoods from 2015 to 2020). Teen pregnancy rates in New York state and New York City rose between 2021 and 2023, the first increase in two decades. New York's congressional delegation wrote to HHS Secretary Kennedy urging reinstatement.

**So what:** This is the W32 item ("HHS canceled 53 of 66 TPP grants") arriving at the delivery layer with names and dollar figures attached. Note the mechanism worth generalizing: a program with two decades of documented outcome improvement was terminated on priority alignment, not evidence. Efficacy data did not protect these grantees, which is the sharpest available counterexample to the "prove outcomes and the money follows" thesis running through the rest of this week.

### Kindergarten vaccine exemptions hit 4.2% as executive order proposes narrowing the childhood schedule
*K-12 Dive · August 19, 2026* · [link](https://www.k12dive.com/news/kindergarten-vaccine-rates-continue-downward-trend-CDC/828160/)

CDC data released August 17 shows 4.2% of kindergartners entered the 2025-26 school year exempt from one or more vaccines, up from 3.6%, or about 155,000 children. Exemption rates rose in 41 states and DC, and 24 states reported exemptions above 5%. Coverage fell for all reported vaccines, ranging from 92% for DTaP to 92.4% for MMR and polio; about 280,000 kindergartners attended school without documentation of completing MMR. National measles cases reached a 35-year high by July 30. President Trump signed an executive order August 10 calling for reducing immunizations to cover 11 diseases rather than the 18 recommended by CDC in 2024, separating MMR into three single-disease shots, and administering vaccines at separate visits. The National Association of School Nurses said in an August 11 statement that conflicting recommendations "create confusion for families, complicate school health practice, and undermine confidence."

**So what:** School nurses are the delivery point for compliance tracking, and separate-visit schedules multiply the number of records a school must chase. Immunization compliance software gets a larger job; school health staffing gets a harder one.

### Most New York children miss recommended developmental screening, and the reason is reimbursement
*Hechinger Report · August 17, 2026* · [link](https://hechingerreport.org/in-new-york-most-kids-miss-recommended-screening-for-autism-speech-delays/)

Just over 36% of parents of young children nationally report having completed a developmental screener in the previous 12 months, per the latest National Survey of Children's Health; New York is at about 28%, ahead of only Nevada, Florida, Arkansas and Missouri. The American Academy of Pediatrics has recommended standardized screening at 9, 18 and 30 months since 2006. Practitioners named payment as the obstacle: "Pediatric clinics often lose money on screening," said Noelle Hause of Zero to Three — "it's not because it's not recommended, it's because it's not getting reimbursed." New York did not allow clinics to bill Medicaid separately for developmental screening until a few years ago, and the rate remains under $20 per screening, which does not always cover cost. The State Health Department issued a memo this month citing the low rate and launched a "Be Their Biggest Champion" campaign. Community-delivered alternatives are scaling: Help Me Grow operates in New York and 29 other states, and documented screenings in Onondaga County rose thirtyfold, from about 150 to more than 4,600 annually since 2019.

**So what:** A clean, generalizable lesson in why a mandated, evidence-backed wellbeing practice still doesn't happen: the reimbursement rate sits below delivery cost. For anyone building screening or early-identification tools, the binding constraint is not clinician awareness or product quality — it is a sub-$20 Medicaid code. Note also where the volume actually grew: libraries, playgroups and farmers' markets, not clinics.

### California schools are returning Proposition 28 arts money they can't spend
*The 74 · August 18, 2026* · [link](https://www.the74million.org/article/californians-voted-to-spend-1-billion-on-k-12-arts-why-are-schools-returning-the-money/)

Proposition 28, approved in 2022, sets aside about $1 billion a year for arts education in California public schools, allocated by enrollment and weighted toward low-income students — LAUSD received about $72 million last year. Schools must spend at least 80% on additional arts staff and return anything unspent after three years. The first three-year period just ended; state data compiled by Create CA shows dozens, if not hundreds, of schools spent nothing. Districts cite two obstacles: a teacher shortage (a Stanford Research Institute study found California needs at least 5,000 new arts teachers) and vague statutory language, including uncertainty about pooling funds across districts to share a teacher and fear of litigation for supplanting existing arts funding — Prop 28's author Austin Beutner has sued LAUSD on exactly that theory. Assembly Bill 2440 would clarify the language and has near-unanimous bipartisan support, opposed by Beutner.

**So what:** A billion dollars a year, statutorily protected, and schools handed it back. Two failure modes worth naming for any wellbeing-funding design: an 80%-on-staff mandate is unspendable when the staff don't exist, and ambiguity plus litigation risk makes districts choose forfeiture over a compliance gamble. Money earmarked for *people* fails differently from money that can buy a service.

### Arizona Supreme Court kills the ESA oversight ballot measure
*Chalkbeat · August 18, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/08/18/arizona-court-kills-voucher-oversight-divides-republicans/)

The Arizona Supreme Court invalidated enough signatures to keep the Protect Education Act — which would have imposed significant oversight and an income cap on the state's universal Empowerment Scholarship Accounts — off the November ballot. **So what:** Universal ESA programs are proving very hard to regulate once established, which keeps a growing pool of public education dollars flowing through channels with limited spend oversight. For wellbeing vendors, ESA-eligible direct-to-family spending is a real and largely unexamined route to market that does not require district procurement.

### Colorado families left $20.2 million of Summer EBT food money unspent
*Chalkbeat · August 14, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/colorado/2026/08/14/more-than-a-third-of-families-didnt-use-summer-ebt-food-benefit-last-year/)

Colorado distributed $57.6 million in Summer EBT benefits last summer to more than 480,000 families of preschool-through-12th-grade students, at $120 per child. State data shows 35% of those students never used the benefit, returning nearly $20.2 million to the federal government — enough to have fed more than 168,000 additional children. Colorado had the lowest usage rate of the 20 states tracked by education policy consultant David Rubel; Missouri distributed roughly the same $57 million and lost only $3.5 million. Causes cited: a separate reusable card families lose or discard, low awareness of automatic eligibility, outdated mailing addresses drawn from prior-year district records, and general confusion about eligibility. Families have 122 days to spend; this year's clock runs out in late September.

**So what:** Benefit *uptake* is a distinct and badly-served problem from benefit *funding* — a third of an allocated, uncontested food benefit evaporated on card logistics and stale addresses. Districts hold the family contact data and the trust relationship, which makes uptake infrastructure a plausible district-adjacent product with an unusually clean ROI story: the money is already appropriated.

### Pennsylvania's statewide cellphone ban is still stalled, so Philadelphia schools are writing their own
*Chalkbeat · August 20, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/philadelphia/2026/08/20/pennsylvania-still-hasnt-passed-a-school-cellphone-ban-some-philly-principals-arent-waiting/)

Pennsylvania's Senate passed a school cellphone ban in February and the House passed an amended version in June, but lawmakers have not agreed on a final version, with disagreement over which schools it covers and what exceptions apply. Gov. Josh Shapiro has called for a ban comparable to New York's and New Jersey's bell-to-bell requirements. In the absence of state law, Philadelphia leaves policy to individual schools: West Philadelphia High School locks phones in a clear classroom cabinet during instruction and returns them at the bell, allowing lunchtime and passing-period use, with teachers able to unlock them for schoolwork. **So what:** The "bell-to-bell versus instructional-time-only" split is the fork that decides whether a school is a buyer of device-management and digital-wellbeing tooling or of pouches and cabinets. Two states have gone bell-to-bell; Pennsylvania is deadlocked, and the FCC is simultaneously asking whether E-rate should require parental screen opt-outs. The same question is now live at three levels of government at once.

### Denver's $44 million ballot measure earmarks $2.9M for mental health and $2M for special education
*Chalkbeat · August 14, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/colorado/2026/08/14/denver-voters-to-decide-on-44-million-mill-levy-override-for-public-schools/)

The Denver school board voted unanimously to place a $44 million mill levy override on the November 3 ballot, raising property taxes about $72 a year on a median $624,800 home. The split: $25.8 million for a flat $1,750 raise for every full-time DPS employee, $4 million for career and technical education programming, **$2.9 million to expand student mental health support, $2 million for special education services**, and $9.3 million to district charter schools as state law requires. DPS serves about 89,000 students on a $1.5 billion budget and cites declining enrollment under per-student funding, rising health insurance and utility costs, and an increase in students needing special education services. Denver last passed a mill levy override in 2020, for $32 million, similarly earmarked for salaries, special education, mental health and school nurses.

**So what:** A repeat pattern worth noting: Denver's 2020 override funded the same categories, and voters approved it. Mill levy overrides are becoming the reliable funding source for mental health and special education capacity that formula funding no longer covers — and unlike ESSER, they are recurring and locally controlled. The dollar figures are small but the durability profile is good.

### Chicago's board sends two advisory revenue referendums to voters after betting its budget on $150M in state money
*Chalkbeat · August 13, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/chicago/2026/08/13/chicago-public-schools-board-backs-revenue-referendums/)

For the first time in its history, the Chicago Board of Education voted to place two non-binding questions on the November ballot: one on a statewide millionaires tax for public education, one on a financial penalty on owners of vacant city land with revenue steered to CPS. ### NYC paraprofessional $10,000 bonus becomes law; City Hall sues, $325 million at stake
*Chalkbeat · August 19, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/08/19/nyc-paraprofessionals-10000-pay-bump-law-mamdani/)

A bill guaranteeing New York City paraprofessionals a one-time $10,000 payment became law automatically on Wednesday after Mayor Zohran Mamdani declined to sign or veto it; within hours City Hall announced a legal challenge arguing the legislation improperly bypasses collective bargaining. Paraprofessionals — school aides who typically support students with disabilities — start at roughly $33,000 a year and are among the lowest-paid public school employees. The Council passed the "respect check" 49-0 and estimates the total cost at about $325 million, paid in four installments between January and August 2027 to roughly 25,000 eligible paraprofessionals plus prorated substitutes. UFT President Michael Mulgrew vowed "all-out war." **So what:** A 1,600-person shortage in the role that delivers IEP-mandated one-to-one support is a service-delivery gap a district must fill somehow. The relevant question for vendors is whether districts respond to aide shortages by raising pay, by contracting agency staff, or by substituting technology — this case is a natural experiment in the first option, and its legal outcome sets whether legislatures can mandate school labor costs outside bargaining.

### NYC's redesigned school survey asks 357,000 students about vaping, inclusion and coping — and gets uncomfortable answers
*Chalkbeat · August 17, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/08/17/nyc-2026-school-survey-results-vaping-bathrooms-real-world-learning/)

New York City cut its student school survey from 79 questions to 61 and rewrote them in plainer language; more than 357,000 students in grades 6-12 participated in 2026, a 3-point rise in participation. Results on the new questions: more than a third of students said vaping is common (19% "very often," 16% "often"), rising from 19% of sixth graders to 45% of ninth graders, and reaching nearly three-quarters of students at one high school versus 11% at another. Just **45% said they use strategies learned at school to stay focused when something frustrating happens** — Asian American girls lowest at 37%, Black male students highest at 51%. About 10% never feel included in activities like clubs, sports, recess and field trips, with another 30% only "sometimes"; ninth graders reported feeling excluded most (45%). 86% of students said they can always or mostly use a bathroom matching their gender identity, falling to 65% among students identified as neither male nor female. Officials cautioned that lower favorability reflects more direct question wording, and that 2026 becomes a new baseline rather than a comparison to prior years.

**So what:** A district of this size publishing school-level wellbeing data creates exactly the denominator a vendor needs: 45% coping-strategy uptake is a measurable baseline that an SEL intervention can be held against, and the school-level breakouts identify which buildings have the acute problem. Note the ninth-grade cliff across three separate measures — vaping, exclusion, real-world relevance all peak in the transition year.

### Newark charter network builds a $51 million school plus a wellness center and 70 affordable housing units
*Chalkbeat · August 17, 2026* · [link](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newark/2026/08/17/charter-school-network-to-build-new-south-ward-school-and-community-hub/)

Newark's Central Planning Board approved the BRICK network's $51 million K-8 building for Gateway Academy in May. Gateway's nearly 470 students currently occupy a rented Clinton Avenue building with no windows, gym, or playground; the new Jelliff Avenue facility is slated to open in 2028-29 with capacity for 810 students, a full kitchen, a community room, and a gym open to South Ward sports leagues and nonprofits. Newark Public Schools Superintendent Roger León has been working to slow citywide charter enrollment growth.

**So what:** A charter network vertically integrating health services and housing around a school is the wraparound-services model with a capital budget behind it. Where a district would procure a telehealth vendor, this operator is building the clinic — worth watching as a competing structure for delivering the same student services.

### An Oceanside, California school names its SEL stack: Move This World, Riverside Insights' DESSA, Stepping Stones Group
*K-12 Dive · August 17, 2026* · [link](https://www.k12dive.com/news/how-a-california-school-is-streamlining-its-approach-to-sel-supports/827968/)

Foussat Language Academy, a 700-student K-5 public magnet in California's Oceanside Unified School District with a single counselor, piloted a multi-tiered system of supports model for SEL beginning in February 2026 and is launching it fully this school year. ### Two new benchmarks find AI tutors degrade over time, and education-specialized models don't lead
*EdTech Insiders · August 13, 2026* · [link](https://edtechinsiders.substack.com/p/new-tutoring-benchmarks-continue)

Two research benchmarks were released. EduClaw-Bench evaluates AI-agent configurations over a 30-day learning horizon using simulated learners built from real knowledge-tracing data, and found that almost no model or agent combination studied — including GPT-5.5 and Alibaba's Qwen models — maintained strong tutoring performance over time; the authors argue sustained tutoring requires learner modeling, orchestration and "pedagogical memory." ELBench evaluated nine models (seven general-purpose frontier systems, two education-specialized) across general capability, safety and trustworthiness, basic education, and higher-order educational development; the two education-specialized models failed to lead in either education-specific module. The newsletter notes Digital Promise's first K-12 Infrastructure program grants include funding for benchmarks, and that most edtech companies are not benchmarking their own products against any research-released benchmark.

**So what:** Read alongside ED's guidance the same week: the federal government just told districts to buy on evidence of effectiveness, and the available evidence says single-turn quality doesn't predict 30-day performance and that being "education-specialized" confers no measured advantage. For a wellbeing product with a conversational component, durability over weeks — not demo quality — is the thing to be able to prove.

## Ideas & Influence Layer

### Common Sense Media: 70% of teens use AI for schoolwork, 30% have ever discussed safe use with a teacher
*The 74 · August 18, 2026* · [link](https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-5-ways-ai-is-cutting-into-students-ability-to-learn/)

A nationally representative Common Sense Media survey of 1,017 U.S. teens aged 13-17, conducted in April and May, found 70% use AI for schoolwork while only 30% say a teacher has ever talked with them about using it safely and 26% about evaluating whether AI output is accurate. Among AI users, 77% describe using it as a support tool and 66% say it helps them understand rather than just finish work — but 63% report going straight to a chatbot for an answer, of whom 25% use the output as-is. On cognitive effects, 38% say having AI available leads them to develop fewer original ideas and 39% feel they are missing out on learning when they use it to complete assignments. School blocking largely fails: of the 44% who have had an AI tool blocked on a school device or network, 59% simply switched to a personal device with less oversight. Asked which skills matter most for their future, teens chose working well with others (55%), fact-checking and critical thinking (50%), and reading and understanding complex information (48%). A College Board survey last fall put high schoolers' AI use for schoolwork at 84%. Common Sense launched a Digital Literacy & Well-Being Curriculum for K-8 and high school alongside the findings, plus an AI and Student Well-Being professional development course.

**So what:** Two procurement-relevant findings. First, blocking doesn't work and pushes use onto unmonitored personal devices — which argues against filtering products and for instruction and disclosure. Second, 38-39% of teens self-report cognitive and motivational costs, which is the first widely-cited dataset framing AI use as a *student wellbeing* issue rather than an academic integrity one. Expect that framing to shape district AI policy, and note that Common Sense has already shipped curriculum into the gap it documented.

### Trevor Project and GLISTEN: affirming school environments correlate with higher grades and belonging
*The 74 · August 20, 2026* · [link](https://www.the74million.org/article/higher-grades-and-belonging-trans-students-thrive-when-supported-by-teachers/)

A report from GLISTEN and the Trevor Project found that transgender and nonbinary students whose identities are affirmed at school report a greater sense of belonging, higher grades, and fuller class participation, with similar outcomes where students can connect with LGBTQ+ peers, see positive representation in classrooms, or attend schools with inclusive policies on sports and facilities. More than half of trans and nonbinary students said their school had implemented at least one policy or practice discriminatory toward LGBTQ+ students, and roughly 60% reported at least one negative event at school — 49% verbal harassment related to their identity and 27% being barred from dressing in accordance with their gender. "School environments can play a powerful role in determining whether transgender and nonbinary students thrive or struggle at school," said Derrick Matthews, director of research science at the Trevor Project.

**So what:** Pair this with the NYC survey's finding that 65% of students identified as neither male nor female can use a bathroom matching their gender identity, versus 86% overall. Two independent datasets the same week measuring the same gap, in a policy environment moving the other way — the evidence base and the regulatory direction are diverging, which is the condition under which district-level variation, not federal policy, determines what gets bought.
